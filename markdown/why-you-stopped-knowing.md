# Why You Stopped Knowing What's Happening in Your Own Company

> There is a size at which a founder stops seeing the work and starts receiving descriptions of it. Somewhere past forty people, and certainly by eighty, direct observation stops scaling. What replaces it — status decks, weekly reviews, dashboards, project tools — are records of reporting rather than records of work. This paper sets out why each substitute fails structurally, why buying a tool that generates work usually makes it worse, and the three properties a record has to have to be worth trusting.

## The crossing

At twelve people you know what everyone is doing because you are in the room. At twenty-five you can still reconstruct it in a morning of walking around. Past forty that stops working — not because the team got worse or the founder got lazier, but because direct observation has a ceiling.

What replaces observation is description. From that point on, almost everything a founder knows about their own company arrives as an account of the work: a slide assembled last night, a sentence in a review meeting, a number on a dashboard whose definition nobody has checked, a date in a project tool last edited in March.

The usual diagnoses are wrong and expensive. It is not that managers are hiding things, that the team lacks ownership, or that the founder should be more hands-on. The problem is that the act of recording work has been separated from the act of doing it. Everything else follows from that split.

## The four substitutes, and what each actually measures

**The weekly review** measures what people chose to raise in a room containing their peers and their boss. Selection is the whole mechanism, and the items most worth hearing are the ones nobody wants to be the person who raises.

**The status deck** measures the assembly effort of whoever built it, usually the night before, usually by asking several people who each reconstructed their week from memory. It reaches the founder after two rounds of summarisation.

**The dashboard** measures whatever the underlying systems happen to capture, which is rarely the work and often only its financial shadow. Dashboards default to green because thresholds require somebody to decide what bad looks like.

**The project tool** measures how recently someone updated the project tool. Updating it competes for time with the work it describes, so it is maintained best when the team is idle and worst when the team is under pressure — precisely when it is needed.

All four are produced on a schedule, by a person, for an audience. That is the definition of reporting, and it carries a bias no amount of good faith removes.

## Why buying a better tool usually makes it worse

Most tools bought to fix this generate work: recurring tasks, reminders, alerts, digests. The pitch is that the system keeps everyone honest. The mechanism is that the system manufactures items and assumes people clear them.

Generated items accumulate faster than any human clears them — the generator runs on a schedule, the human runs on available attention. Once the queue passes the length that can be read in a sitting, it stops being read, and the tool begins reporting on itself.

In one live system, a single user's generated action queue grew from 389 open items to 541 to 583, then sat at 583 unchanged for nineteen days, with every reminder digest in that period unopened. Nothing was broken. The system worked as designed — it had been generating obligations for longer than anyone could absorb them, and the person on the other end had rationally stopped looking.

The useful question is not how to clear the backlog. It is whether a backlog should have been possible.

## What a record worth trusting has to do

**One: it is a byproduct, not a task.** If recording is a separate job it competes with the work and loses, permanently. The only version that survives is one where a person writes their line once, where they already are, and every other view reads that same line — not a synchronised copy. Two copies means two screens that can disagree, and once they do, both lose authority.

**Two: absence reads as absence.** If a day with no record becomes an item in a queue, you have built a backlog. If it stays empty, you have built a measurement: work was recorded on this many of the available working days. That number is honest, cheap, and comparable across people, provided the denominator comes from their actual working calendar rather than a count of rows.

**Three: closing requires evidence, not a declaration.** A step marked done by the person responsible for it is an assertion, and assertions are what the problem is made of. Attach a file, a link, or a note that cannot be quietly rewritten, and the record stops depending on memory or optimism. The same applies to money: a spend ledger that can be edited is not a ledger, and a correction that overwrites the original destroys the one thing worth keeping.

The test: if everyone stopped trying to keep you informed tomorrow, would you still know what happened this week?

## Five things to check on Monday

1. **Pick a project and ask when its dates were last changed.** Not when it was last discussed — when the plan itself was edited. Dates that have moved repeatedly with no record of why are a rolling forecast, not a plan.
2. **Ask what someone in a non-revenue function did last Tuesday.** If the only way to answer is to ask them, their work is invisible by default and unassessable in either direction.
3. **Open the queue in whichever tool sends the most reminders.** Count open items, check the age of the oldest, check whether recent digests were opened.
4. **Find something marked complete last quarter and ask what proves it.** An owner marking it complete is a declaration. A document, sign-off or measurement attached to it is a record.
5. **Ask where the last three project decisions were actually made.** If the honest answer is a messaging group, that is the system of record, and any tool that ignores it will only ever be a second place people are asked to type things.

## What GoWarm Work does, and what it refuses to do

Projects carry stages, gates, dependencies and a plan frozen at the start, so drift is a measured difference rather than a monthly renegotiation. Tasks need evidence attached before closing. Bills of quantity behave like ledgers, with corrections booked as reversals and approved variations kept separate from the original bill.

Daily work is one line a day, written by the person doing the work, stored in exactly one place and read by their own view, by the project task, and by their manager. Nothing generates an entry, so there is no backlog. A day with no entry is a day nobody worked on it, measured against the working calendar.

It will not count hours, take screenshots, detect idleness or produce a productivity score. Those products answer a different question — whether people are working, rather than what the company is doing — and that question corrodes what it measures. A record people are afraid of is a record nobody writes honestly.
