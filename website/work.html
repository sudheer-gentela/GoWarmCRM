GoWarm Work · Projects and Daily Work

You built the company. Then it got big enough that you stopped seeing the work.

Not the results. The work. Who is actually doing what today, which projects are moving, which quietly stopped three weeks ago and nobody said. GoWarm Work is built so the record of that answers itself, because writing it is part of doing the job rather than a second job on top of it.

Written once

A person writes one line about what they did on a piece of work. It shows on their day, against the project task, and in their manager's view. Nobody retypes it anywhere.

Nothing to fall behind on

No system generates work for anyone. A day with nothing logged is simply a day nobody worked on it, so there is no queue that grows past the point of being read.

Runs without a project office

Gates, dependencies, a frozen plan and required evidence do the work an administrator would otherwise be doing. Under 500 people, there is no administrator.

Everything you were sold to fix this reports on the reporting.

The weekly review tells you what people chose to raise. The status deck tells you what someone assembled the night before. The dashboard is green because green is the default and nobody set the threshold. The project tool has last month's dates in it because updating it is a separate task that competes with the work.

None of these is a record of work. Each is a record of someone describing work, produced on a schedule, by a person with a reason to describe it a particular way.

This is not a discipline problem and it does not get better by asking harder. It is what happens when the act of recording is separated from the act of working. Separate them, and the record decays at exactly the speed the work speeds up — which is why it is always worst in the quarter you most need it.

The correction is structural. Put the writing where the work already is, make the missing case obvious instead of silent, and stop letting anyone close something by declaring it closed. The longer argument is here.

Two halves, one record.

Projects hold the shape of the work. Daily work holds what actually happened. They are joined at the task, so a person answers once and three different people get their answer.

The join is the whole design. Two stored copies would need synchronising, and the first time two screens disagree, both stop being believed.

Projects

For anything with a shape: an implementation, a site, a build, a migration, an audit, a retainer, a standing capability that will never finish.

  • Stages, with gates that hold the next stage shut until the current one is genuinely met
  • Task dependencies, checked for circularity when you create them rather than when you are late
  • A baseline frozen when the project starts, so drift is a number instead of an argument
  • Evidence attached to the task before it closes — a file, a link, a note that cannot be quietly rewritten
  • Timeboxed and standing modes, so a retainer is not a project permanently at 90%
  • Bill of Quantities with an append-only spend ledger, rate snapshots and corrections booked as reversals

Daily work

For the half of the company whose contribution never appears in any deal, ticket or dashboard, and who currently live in a shared spreadsheet.

  • One short line a day per piece of work, written by the person doing it
  • Recurring work that never completes, and assigned work that completes once
  • A controlled list of activity types, so three spellings of the same thing do not become three lines in the rollup
  • An "Other" escape hatch that never blocks anyone, feeding a queue the manager promotes or merges from
  • Logging measured against working days and your holiday calendar, not against tasks somebody generated
  • A manager view that puts each person's logging beside their open and overdue project tasks

The baseline is frozen when the project starts and never re-fitted, so the gap between plan and reality is a number anyone can read rather than a monthly renegotiation.

Four decisions that make the difference.

Most work tools differ in features. These are differences in what the numbers mean, which is the part you cannot fix later with configuration.

The text lives in exactly one row

When someone posts an update on a project task, that update is their daily work entry. Not a copy of it. The same line is read by their own day view, by the task on the project, and by their manager's rollup. Edit it from any of the three and you have edited the one row.

Why it matters: the standard alternative is to store the text twice and synchronise. Synchronisation drifts, and once it drifts, two screens disagree and both lose their authority. Nobody trusts the tool after that, and the spreadsheet comes back.

Nothing is generated, so nothing can accumulate

No scheduler creates a daily entry for anyone. A day with no entry is an absence, not an unfinished item. Compliance is the number of days a person logged divided by the working days in the window, and the denominator comes from their schedule and your holiday calendar.

Why it matters: in one live system we looked at, a single user's generated action queue had climbed from 389 to 541 to 583 open items and then sat unchanged for nineteen days, with every reminder unread. That is the resting state of every tool that manufactures work. A table that only gains a row when a human writes one cannot get there.

Generated items arrive on a schedule; attention does not. The only queue that cannot outrun its reader is one nothing adds to automatically.

Not everything is supposed to finish

Work has two independent questions: whose is it, and does it end. Customer or internal is one axis. Timeboxed or standing is the other. All four combinations are real, and a tool that collapses them into one list will misreport at least half of your work.

Why it matters: tools that only model finite projects force the standing half into a permanently open item, which then pollutes every count you look at. Standing work here is retired, not completed, and stays out of the statistics that are about delivery.

Both rows are ordinary work. Only the top row fits a conventional project tool, which is why the bottom row ends up in a spreadsheet or nowhere at all.

Money sits on the same object as the work

The Bill of Quantities is a first-class part of a project, not an export. Progress entries are increments rather than running totals. Every entry snapshots the rate it was booked at. A mistake is corrected by a reversal that points at the original rather than by an edit. Approved variations stay separate from the original bill.

Why it matters: fold a variation into the original quantities and the project looks on budget while the reason it grew disappears. Allow edits to a spend ledger and it stops being a ledger. This is ordinary practice in construction and almost absent from software-first project tools.

What people actually see.

Four screens carry the whole product. Two belong to the person doing the work, two to whoever is accountable for it.

Most of your project history is in a messaging group.

Under 500 people, that is simply true, and pretending otherwise is why so many rollouts fail. Decisions get made in a group thread, a photograph of a site or a shipment is the only record that it happened, and the project tool learns about it a week later if at all.

GoWarm Work lets an administrator attach the conversations your teams already use to the project they belong to, so that history lands against the right piece of work instead of scrolling away. Connections are set up by your organisation, with your consent, on the channels you choose — you decide what is in scope and what stays out.

What this is not: it is not monitoring people, and it is not a timer. There is no screenshot capture, no idle detection and no hour counting anywhere in the product. The measure a manager gets is whether work was recorded on a working day. That is a deliberately low bar, and it is the only one that survives contact with a busy team.

Questions we get asked

Do we need the sales modules to use this?

No. Modules are enabled per organisation. You can run projects and daily work with every sales module switched off, and turn them on later without migrating anything.

How is this different from Jira, Asana or Microsoft Project?

Those assume somebody maintains them. This assumes nobody does. The discipline is in the structure: a task cannot close without evidence, a gate holds a stage shut, the plan is frozen at start so drift is measurable, and a person writes one line rather than updating three systems.

Will people actually log anything?

The bar is one line a day, written where they already are, with a controlled list to pick from and an escape hatch that never blocks them. It is a habit, and habits need a low floor. Anything heavier is abandoned in week three and you know that already.

Is this surveillance?

No. No timers, no screenshots, no activity monitoring, no idle detection. The manager sees whether work was recorded on a working day and what it was. Nothing about how someone spent their hours.

We are not a software company. Does that matter?

It is the reason several of these features exist. A bill of quantities, standing work that never completes and required evidence are not software-team concerns. They came from the companies that use this.

How long does it take to set up?

The setup that matters is small: your departments, working schedules, holiday calendar and a first list of activity types. Most teams are logging within a week. The activity list is meant to be edited in the first month, not perfected before you start.
